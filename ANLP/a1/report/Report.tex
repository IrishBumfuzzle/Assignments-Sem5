\section{Task and approach}
We are given 5000 lines of English text (the Brown corpus, one sentence-line per
line) and their ciphertexts, produced by a repeating-key XOR cipher with the 8-byte
key \texttt{ANLP2026}: byte $i$ of the ciphertext is $C[i] = P[i] \oplus K[i \bmod 8]$.
The ciphertext is provided as a bit string. The task is to learn the mapping
ciphertext $\to$ plaintext with a sequence-to-sequence Transformer built from
scratch in PyTorch (no \texttt{nn.Transformer} or \texttt{nn.MultiheadAttention}),
with a \emph{learned} (BPE) tokenization on the ciphertext side, and to run a
five-way ablation over architectural choices:

\bottomrule
\end{tabular}

All five configurations share the same model budget and training recipe: $d_{model}=256$,
8 heads, 4 encoder + 4 decoder layers, FFN width 1024, dropout 0.1, learning rate
$5\cdot10^{-4}$ (250-step warmup, cosine decay to $10^{-5}$), AdamW with weight decay
$10^{-2}$, effective batch size 16, 40 epochs, maximum source length 1024 tokens.
The evaluation protocol is greedy decoding on 500 held-out test lines, scored with
bit-level accuracy, exact-sequence accuracy, mean Levenshtein distance, and
(character-level) BLEU and ROUGE-L. The full training and ablation pipeline is
logged to Weights \& Biases, and checkpoints are hosted on Hugging Face
(Section~\ref{sec:repro}).

Beyond the ablation itself, the most important empirical finding of this project
is a training/decoding mismatch in the autoregressive configurations: the models
reach 0.93 teacher-forced bit accuracy but only $\approx$0.69 under greedy
decoding, and we document a controlled diagnosis of this exposure-bias gap
(Section~\ref{sec:exposure}) together with three training-side mitigations that we
tested and that all failed. The token-free non-autoregressive configuration C5,
which has no exposure bias by construction, decrypts the test set to 100\% bit
accuracy (93.4\% exact full-line matches), which both confirms the diagnosis and
serves as a strong baseline.

\section{Design decisions}
\label{sec:design}
Two design decisions on the input/target side matter more than any architectural
choice, so we describe them first.

\paragraph{Ciphertext: learned BPE over a phase-annotated byte alphabet.}
The assignment requires a learned subword tokenization of the ciphertext. We apply
byte-level BPE (vocabulary 8000) to the ciphertext \emph{bytes} --- not to fixed
8-bit chunks --- and we enrich the alphabet with the key phase: the symbol at
byte index $i$ is $(b, i \bmod 8)$, a 2048-symbol alphabet. Because the key is
8 bytes long and the phase is a deterministic function of the byte index, this
makes decryption a \emph{per-symbol} lookup: a ciphertext symbol $(b,p)$ decrypts
to $b \oplus K[p]$ independent of every other symbol. Without the phase
annotation the model must recover $i \bmod 8$ from context alone, which is a
strictly harder regression problem for the same budget. BPE still merges
frequently co-occurring $(b,p)$-symbol pairs, so the tokenization is genuinely
learned and variable-length, as required.

\paragraph{Target: one token per plaintext byte.}
The natural first attempt tokenizes the plaintext with a second BPE vocabulary
(ciphertext-BPE $\to$ plaintext-BPE). That configuration \emph{collapses to the
English prior}: after 40 epochs it reaches 0.668 bit accuracy, essentially the
0.662 of an always-space predictor, even though its teacher-forced loss keeps
decreasing. The cause is a resegmentation problem: a BPE token in the ciphertext
covers a variable number of bytes, and a BPE token in the plaintext covers a
variable number of \emph{different} bytes, so the model must first learn an
ordinal alignment between two independent segmentations of the same text. We
measured the overlap of the two BPE boundaries on matched lines (intersection
over union of boundary sets): 0.41 --- the segmentations mostly disagree.
Copy-mechanism controls (predicting the input as-is, and decrypting with a
constant 1-byte key) show that the 4-layer budget is spent on this ordinal
alignment over $\sim$200--1000 positions, not on the cipher itself.

The fix is to remove the resegmentation: the target is one token per plaintext
byte (256-way alphabet plus BOS/EOS), so target position $i$ corresponds exactly
to source byte position $i$ and the alignment is the identity. The cipher becomes
a per-position, per-phase substitution, and the only thing left for the model to
learn is to look at the source symbol at the same index. With byte targets but a
\emph{raw-byte} BPE source (no phase annotation), the model reaches only
$\approx$0.62 bit accuracy in a 25-epoch probe --- the model then has to compute
$i \bmod 8$ from position by itself. With the phase-annotated source the same
probe reaches 0.89--0.90 teacher-forced by epoch 25, and the final full-length
run reaches 0.927 at 40 epochs (Table~\ref{tab:main}). The target-side choice is ours to make; the ciphertext
tokenization remains learned BPE, as the assignment requires.

\paragraph{Training implementation notes.}
Three implementation choices had measurable effects and are worth recording.
(i)~\emph{Activation checkpointing} of all encoder/decoder blocks (numerically
identical) makes the $\approx$2670-position byte targets trainable on an 11\,GB
GPU with batch 8. (ii)~\emph{Random batches, not length-bucketed batches}:
with length-homogeneous buckets, alignment learning stalls at $\approx$0.67 bit
accuracy by epoch 6, while identical data and schedule with shuffled random
batches reach $\approx$0.83 by epoch 6 --- each bucketed optimizer step sees a
single length band, so the model never consolidates one alignment rule across
lengths. (iii)~\emph{Evaluation alignment}: the per-batch loss is unaffected by
line order, but set-level metrics require the references to be permuted to the
DataLoader's iteration order; we verify this by checking that oracle
(teacher-forced) incremental decoding reproduces the teacher-forced evaluation
bit-for-bit.

\section{Autoregressive decoding: the teacher-forced / greedy gap}
\label{sec:exposure}
For C1 (and, weaker, for all of C1--C4) there is a large gap between the
teacher-forced and greedy metrics: at 40 epochs, teacher-forced validation bit
accuracy is 0.927 while greedy decoding scores 0.690 on test (Table~\ref{tab:main}),
and greedy validation accuracy is nearly flat (0.679 at epoch 10 to 0.691 at
epoch 40) while teacher-forced accuracy climbs steadily. Under greedy decoding the
first few bytes of a line are correct and then the model drifts: its own (wrong)
prefix feeds back into the decoder, the cross-attention indexes the wrong region
of the source, and the output degenerates into fluent but unrelated English. This
is the classic exposure bias of autoregressive models, and it is the metric that
the assignment actually scores, so we investigated it carefully.

\paragraph{A controlled prefix sweep isolates the cause.}
We took the epoch-25 C1 checkpoint and ran a 30-line validation sweep in which we
control what the decoder is fed as its own prefix (Table~\ref{tab:prefix_sweep}).
With the \emph{oracle} prefix (ground-truth bytes fed incrementally through the
same KV-cache decoding path the model uses at inference), accuracy is 0.893 ---
matching the teacher-forced evaluation (0.898) and confirming that the decoding
machinery itself is exact. Randomizing 10\% of prefix positions costs 0.10 bit
accuracy; at 100\% random the model is \emph{below} the always-space baseline
(0.654 vs 0.662). Periodically re-anchoring the decoder to the oracle prefix every
$k$ bytes recovers most of the gap for small $k$ (0.767 at $k{=}2$, 0.691 at
$k{=}10$). The interpretation is unambiguous: the model can decrypt, but it uses
the \emph{content} of the prefix to locate the source position, so it stays
aligned only while the prefix it has generated so far is correct. One wrong byte
and it never re-locates; it falls back to its English language-model prior, which
is why the outputs read fluently.

\paragraph{Random prefix dropout does not fix it.}
The standard training-side remedy is to corrupt the fed prefix during training so
the model learns to be robust to its own mistakes. We replaced a fraction $q$ of
the decoder-input prefix ids with random ids. $q{=}0.5$ barely moves greedy
(0.679) and noticeably hurts teacher forcing (0.774 at epoch 15 vs $\approx$0.89
baseline); $q{=}1.0$ removes the bootstrap signal entirely and the model collapses
to the English prior (0.662/0.657, Table~\ref{tab:fixes}). The reason is visible
in the sweep: random dropout teaches the model to ignore \emph{obviously wrong}
(random) bytes, but greedy failure comes from \emph{plausible} wrong bytes ---
valid English that simply does not match this line. Robustness to random noise is
not robustness to fluent drift.

\paragraph{Scheduled sampling does not fix it either.}
The natural distribution to sample from is the model's own output: every 4 epochs
we greedy-decode the training set with the current weights, cache the predicted
prefixes, and --- with probability ramping $0 \to 0.5$ over 8 epochs --- feed each
sample its own cached prefix as the decoder input while supervising against the
true target. A 15-epoch probe shows it also does not help: greedy stays at 0.664
(below the 0.68 of the plain teacher-forced baseline) while teacher-forced
accuracy drops to 0.814 (Table~\ref{tab:fixes}). Training against \emph{any}
corrupted fraction of the prefix makes the model distrust a correct prefix too ---
the alignment signal is weakened without the model ever learning to re-locate the
source position from a wrong one.

\paragraph{Conclusion.}
Within the fixed architecture and the greedy-decoding evaluation protocol, the
exposure-bias gap is not closed by input-side corruption under any tested
distribution (random bytes at $q \in \{0.5, 1.0\}$, or the model's own greedy
output). The official runs therefore use plain teacher forcing, which gives both
the best teacher-forced accuracy and the best greedy accuracy (0.68--0.69). We
note for completeness that the gap would disappear with non-greedy decoding
(beam search or sampling with resampling, which the evaluation protocol does not
allow) --- and, as C5 shows, with a non-autoregressive target.

\section{Setup}
\label{sec:setup}
\textbf{Data.} 5000 Brown-corpus sentence lines with their ciphertexts, split
80/10/10 with seed 42 (4000 train / 500 val / 500 test). Ciphertext bit strings
are decoded to bytes; plaintext lengths range from 21 to 2670 bytes (median
554). For C5 the raw-byte source uses one token per byte, so lines whose
ciphertext exceeds the 1024-token source cap are excluded rather than truncated
(4363 of 5000 lines retained; test set 426 lines).

\textbf{Hardware and software.} One NVIDIA RTX 2080 Ti (11\,GB, compute
capability 7.5). PyTorch 2.x with half-precision training: fp16 autocast with a
gradient scaler on this Turing GPU (the code auto-selects bf16 without a scaler
on Ampere and newer). Normalization layers compute their statistics in fp32
internally for fp16 safety. One config takes 2.2--2.5 hours (40 epochs); C5 takes
15 minutes.

\textbf{Model.} $d_{model}=256$, 8 heads (C3: 4 key/value heads), 4 encoder + 4
decoder layers, FFN 1024, dropout 0.1. Parameter counts: C1/C2 9.54M, C4 9.54M,
C3 9.02M, C5 3.39M.

\textbf{Training.} AdamW ($\beta_1{=}0.9$, $\beta_2{=}0.98$, weight decay
$10^{-2}$), peak lr $5\cdot10^{-4}$ with 250-step linear warmup and cosine decay
to $10^{-5}$, gradient clipping at 1.0, effective batch 16 (batch 8 with 2
gradient-accumulation steps; C5 batch 16), 40 epochs. Max source length 1024
tokens; target length is set to the longest plaintext in the splits
($\approx$2672 for byte targets). Batches are randomly shuffled (not length
bucketed; Section~\ref{sec:design}).

\textbf{Evaluation.} Every epoch: teacher-forced evaluation on the validation set
(bit accuracy, sequence accuracy, Levenshtein, BLEU, ROUGE-L) --- cheap and stable
for the long byte-target sequences, and used to select the best checkpoint by
validation loss. Additionally, full greedy decoding on validation every 10 epochs,
and a final greedy pass on the 500-line test set with the best checkpoint, which
is what Table~\ref{tab:main} reports. All runs are logged to a Weights \& Biases
project (\texttt{anlp-assignment1}, team \texttt{irishbumfuzzle-team}) and the
best checkpoint of each config is uploaded to a per-config Hugging Face
repository (\texttt{anlp-a1-C1}--\texttt{anlp-a1-C5}).

Three readings of Table~\ref{tab:main}.

First, among the autoregressive subword configs, the three architectural changes
that leave the base untouched --- RoPE (C2), GQA (C3), RMSNorm (C4) --- have very
different effects: GQA and RMSNorm are quality-neutral (0.6905 / 0.6902 vs the
0.6902 base; GQA is in fact marginally best on Levenshtein and BLEU), while RoPE
is clearly worse on every metric (0.6773 bit, BLEU 0.184) --- discussed below.

Second, all four autoregressive configs sit within $\pm$0.014 of each other and
about 0.24 below their own teacher-forced accuracy (0.925--0.927). This is the
exposure-bias ceiling of Section~\ref{sec:exposure}: the architectural axis of the
ablation moves the metric by at most $\approx$0.015, while the
decoding/alignment axis costs 0.24. Sequence accuracy is near zero for
C1--C4 (0.2--0.6\% of lines exactly right) --- a single drifted byte in a
500-byte line already loses exact match --- and this is the same quantity that
C5 gets right for 93.4\% of lines, which is the cleanest possible confirmation
that the remaining error is alignment maintenance, not cipher learning.

Third, C5 (token-free, non-autoregressive, 1:1 byte mapping) is the strongest
result of the study: 1.0000 bit accuracy, 0.9343 exact sequences, mean edit
distance 0.1, with 3.4M parameters and a 15-minute training run. The 6.6\% of
lines that are not exact contain small localized errors (a handful of bytes each)
rather than global drift.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}
\paragraph{RoPE is the wrong inductive bias for this task.}
C2 is a genuine negative result. Its teacher-forced accuracy plateaus at 0.875
(the best checkpoint is at epoch 18, and validation loss then creeps upward while
train loss keeps falling --- the model memorizes but does not generalize further),
and its greedy decoding degenerates into repetition loops: predicted lines such as
``\textit{The film s continue to previous special special special special
special...}'' and ``\textit{the Australian continued the Australian continued the
Australian continued...}'' --- a handful of in-context words extracted from the
source, then a collapsed n-gram loop. The likely reason is what the positional
encoding has to do for this task. Decryption requires \emph{absolute}
target$\leftrightarrow$source position alignment over hundreds of positions:
output position $i$ must read source position $i$. Absolute sinusoidal codes give
every position a stable identity in both sequences, so the model can implement
"read the same index" directly, and crucially the decoder still knows where it is
even when its own prefix is wrong. RoPE encodes only \emph{relative} offsets;
the decoder's absolute position must then be inferred by integrating its (possibly
already wrong) prefix, which is exactly the step that fails under greedy decoding
at length. We therefore read C2 as evidence that, for alignment-heavy
long-sequence tasks with a strict position-index correspondence, absolute
positional encodings are the safer choice --- the reverse of the current default
in general-purpose LLMs, where relative encoding wins because the mapping is
semantic, not positional.

\paragraph{GQA and RMSNorm are free.}
C3 (grouped-query attention, 4 key/value heads) matches the 8-head MHA base on
every metric (0.6905 vs 0.6902 bit; 311.2 vs 313.8 Levenshtein; 0.6921 vs 0.6870
BLEU) with 5.5\% fewer parameters (9.02M vs 9.54M) and a smaller KV cache, which
is the standard inference-time win of GQA with no quality cost at this budget.
C4 (RMSNorm instead of LayerNorm) is indistinguishable in bit accuracy (0.6902)
with a slightly worse Levenshtein (321.4 vs 313.8); RMSNorm's lower constant
cost makes it the sensible default for production, and we find no reason to
prefer LayerNorm here.

\paragraph{The token-free baseline wins, and that is informative.}
C5 pairs each ciphertext byte (patch of 4 raw bytes) with the plaintext byte at
the same index and predicts all positions in parallel. It works because the XOR
cipher is a deterministic per-byte function of $(b, i \bmod 8)$ and the phase is
a deterministic function of the position: once the 1:1 alignment is given, each
output position is a local lookup with context, and there is no autoregressive
prefix to drift. Two caveats belong with this reading. The cipher is far easier
than a general seq2seq objective --- a non-autoregressive model would not
necessarily beat an autoregressive one on, say, open translation, where the
target has its own fluent continuation distribution. And C5's test set is the
426 lines whose ciphertext fits the 1024-token raw-byte source (long lines are
excluded), whereas C1--C4 score all 500; its margin is so large (1.0000 vs
0.69) that this does not change any conclusion, but it is stated for fairness.
What C5 does settle is the attribution of C1--C4's error: the subword models
have already learned the cipher (0.927 teacher-forced), so their 0.69 test score
is not a cipher-learning failure but an alignment-maintenance failure under
autoregressive decoding --- the exposure-bias gap of
Section~\ref{sec:exposure}.

\paragraph{The English-prior collapse, and where the budget runs out.}
The fully-tokenized configuration (BPE on both sides) never leaves the
always-space baseline (0.668 vs 0.662) despite steadily decreasing
teacher-forced loss: the model fits the \emph{distribution} of English while
failing the line-specific mapping. The boundary-overlap measurement
(IoU 0.41 between the two BPE segmentations) and the copy/constant-key controls
identify where the 4-layer budget goes: ordinal alignment between two
independent segmentations of $\sim$200--1000 positions, not the cipher itself.
Removing the resegmentation (byte targets) is the single largest improvement of
the study (0.668 $\to$ 0.927 teacher-forced), and the phase-annotated source
alphabet is the next largest (0.62 $\to$ 0.90 in 25-epoch probes).

\paragraph{Limitations.}
(1)~All runs are single-seed; the C1--C4 spread is small enough that seed
variance would not change the ablation ordering, but exact numbers may move in
the second decimal. (2)~Evaluation is greedy decoding only, per the assignment
protocol; the exposure-bias gap is therefore a property of the scored metric, and
we do not score beam search or sampling. (3)~The byte-level target is a
target-side design choice; the ciphertext tokenization remains learned BPE as
required, and the BLEU/ROUGE-L figures for C1--C4 are character-level,
dominated by the fluent-wrong outputs. (4)~C5 excludes lines longer than the
raw-byte source cap (Section~\ref{sec:results}). (5)~The RoPE negative result is
specific to this alignment-heavy task and length range; we do not claim it
generalizes to semantic seq2seq.
